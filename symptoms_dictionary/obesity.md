# Obesity

Obesity is a chronic, complex disease defined by excess adiposity sufficient to impair health, conventionally identified by a body mass index (BMI) ≥30 kg/m² in adults, with abdominal obesity additionally characterised by waist circumference exceeding 102 cm in men or 88 cm in women using WHO thresholds.

**Cardinal symptoms:** Obesity does not produce a specific symptom cluster in isolation; instead, it is identified through anthropometric measurement. The clinical impact manifests through its comorbidities and mechanical consequences. Dyspnoea on exertion and reduced exercise tolerance are common early functional complaints related to increased metabolic demand, restricted chest wall movement, and frequent obstructive sleep apnoea.

**Associated symptoms and signs:** Snoring and witnessed apnoeas suggest obstructive sleep apnoea, present in a large proportion of individuals with obesity. Excessive daytime sleepiness and unrefreshing sleep follow from sleep fragmentation. Joint pain — particularly in the knees, hips, and lumbar spine — reflects increased mechanical load. Skin changes include intertrigo in skinfold areas, acanthosis nigricans (suggesting insulin resistance), and skin tags. Heartburn and reflux symptoms are common due to increased intra-abdominal pressure. Women may experience irregular menstruation and features of polycystic ovary syndrome.

**Diagnostic features:** BMI ≥30 kg/m² is the primary diagnostic criterion, calculated from directly measured (not self-reported) weight and height. Waist circumference >102 cm in men or >88 cm in women confirms central adiposity with independent cardiovascular risk. In Asian populations, a BMI threshold of ≥27.5 kg/m² is used for obesity per WHO Asia-Pacific guidance. Body composition assessment (DEXA, bioimpedance) resolves ambiguity when BMI is elevated due to muscle mass rather than fat mass.

**Predisposing factors:** Positive energy balance sustained over time is the proximate cause. Contributory factors include ultra-processed food environments, sedentary occupational and leisure patterns, genetic predisposition (polygenic; rare monogenic causes exist), and certain medications — corticosteroids, antipsychotics (particularly olanzapine, clozapine), antiepileptics (valproate), and insulin. Socioeconomic disadvantage, food insecurity, and urbanisation are strong population-level drivers in low- and middle-income countries.

**Typical presentation:** Obesity rarely presents as an isolated chief complaint. It is typically identified as a comorbidity or risk factor when a patient presents with hypertension, type 2 diabetes, dyslipidaemia, musculoskeletal pain, or cardiovascular disease. It may also emerge in the context of investigating infertility, obstructive sleep apnoea, or metabolic liver disease.

**Important differential diagnoses:** Secondary causes of obesity must be excluded when clinical features suggest an underlying disorder. Hypothyroidism produces weight gain alongside fatigue, cold intolerance, constipation, and bradycardia; thyroid function tests are diagnostic. Cushing syndrome produces central adiposity with proximal myopathy, violaceous striae, easy bruising, hypertension, and glucose intolerance; 24-hour urinary free cortisol or overnight dexamethasone suppression test is the initial investigation. Polycystic ovary syndrome in women presents with weight gain, oligomenorrhoea, hyperandrogenism, and insulin resistance. Drug-induced weight gain should be identified by medication history review. Rare genetic causes — Prader-Willi syndrome, leptin deficiency, MC4R mutations — present in childhood with severe early-onset obesity and distinctive clinical features.

**Features that argue against this diagnosis:** Elevated BMI in an athlete with high muscle mass may not represent excess adiposity — body composition assessment resolves this. Weight elevation from fluid accumulation (cardiac oedema, nephrotic syndrome, hepatic ascites) increases measured weight without adiposity and should be distinguished clinically. Rapid unexplained weight gain over weeks without a clear dietary or behavioural explanation argues against primary obesity and should prompt investigation for secondary causes including hypothyroidism, Cushing syndrome, and fluid retention.

**Red flags:** Rapid recent weight gain without an obvious dietary or behavioural explanation should prompt investigation for secondary causes (Cushing, hypothyroidism, medication effect, fluid accumulation from cardiac or renal disease). Obesity hypoventilation syndrome (Pickwickian syndrome) — daytime hypercapnia and hypoxia in an obese individual — is a medical emergency if severe. Features of severe obstructive sleep apnoea with cardiovascular consequences require urgent sleep study and cardiological assessment.

**Diagnostic context:** Diagnosis is primarily anthropometric. BMI is calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m²); WHO defines overweight as 25.0–29.9 and obesity as ≥30 kg/m². For Asian populations, adjusted cut-offs (overweight ≥23, obesity ≥27.5) may be more appropriate for metabolic risk. Waist circumference provides complementary assessment of central adiposity and cardiovascular risk independent of BMI. Investigation should include fasting glucose and HbA1c, lipid profile, thyroid function, liver enzymes (metabolic-associated steatotic liver disease is common), and blood pressure measurement.
