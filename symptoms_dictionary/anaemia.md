# Iron deficiency anaemia

Iron deficiency anaemia (IDA) is the most common nutritional deficiency disorder worldwide, resulting from inadequate iron for haemoglobin synthesis due to insufficient dietary intake, chronic blood loss, impaired absorption, or increased demand, producing a microcytic hypochromic anaemia. In East Africa, anaemia is frequently multifactorial, with iron deficiency, malaria, hookworm infestation, HIV, and haemoglobinopathies often co-existing.

**Cardinal symptoms:** Fatigue and weakness are the most consistent features, resulting from impaired oxygen delivery to tissues. Pallor — of the conjunctivae, oral mucosa, and palmar creases — is the key clinical sign and the most reliable bedside indicator of anaemia. Dyspnoea on exertion and reduced exercise tolerance reflect reduced haemoglobin-mediated oxygen transport.

**Associated symptoms and signs:** Tachycardia at rest or on minimal exertion develops to compensate for reduced oxygen-carrying capacity. Palpitations and lightheadedness may be reported. Specific to iron deficiency: pica (craving for non-food substances — ice, chalk, clay, raw rice) is a characteristic but underreported feature; koilonychia (spoon-shaped nails), glossitis, and angular stomatitis (painful cracking at the corners of the mouth) reflect mucosal epithelial iron dependence. Brittle or ridged nails occur with chronic deficiency. In severe anaemia, a systolic flow murmur from high-output cardiac physiology may be audible.

**Diagnostic features:** Haemoglobin below WHO thresholds confirms anaemia: <13 g/dL in men, <12 g/dL in non-pregnant women, <11 g/dL in pregnant women, <11.5 g/dL in children 5–11 years. Low MCV (<80 fL) and low MCH (<27 pg) indicate microcytic hypochromic anaemia consistent with iron deficiency. Serum ferritin <30 μg/L is the most specific marker of depleted iron stores in the absence of inflammation; ferritin <100 μg/L with an elevated CRP is used when concurrent inflammation is present. Low serum iron, elevated total iron-binding capacity (TIBC), and transferrin saturation <16% confirm functional iron deficiency. Reticulocyte count rise within 5–10 days of commencing oral iron therapy is a functional confirmatory test.

**Predisposing factors for iron deficiency:** Chronic blood loss is the dominant cause in adults — gastrointestinal sources (peptic ulcer, colorectal cancer, hookworm infestation, oesophageal varices) or menstrual blood loss (menorrhagia) should be actively sought. Inadequate dietary iron intake is the primary cause in infants and young children and in populations with predominantly plant-based diets lacking haem iron. Increased demand during pregnancy, lactation, and adolescent growth spurts. Impaired absorption in coeliac disease, post-gastrectomy, or chronic use of proton pump inhibitors.

**Typical presentation:** IDA develops insidiously over months and patients may tolerate remarkably low haemoglobin concentrations — often below 7 g/dL — because of gradual physiological compensation. Many patients present only when anaemia is severe or when a complication (dyspnoea, chest pain, heart failure) brings them to care. In resource-limited settings, a presenting complaint of fatigue, pallor, and exertional breathlessness in a woman of reproductive age frequently represents menorrhagia-associated IDA, while the same presentation in a man or post-menopausal woman should prompt urgent investigation for gastrointestinal blood loss.

**Important differential diagnoses:** The differential of anaemia is broad and should be approached through the full blood count and blood film morphology before jumping to IDA. Anaemia of chronic disease (normocytic or mildly microcytic) is associated with chronic inflammation — HIV, TB, rheumatoid arthritis, malignancy; serum ferritin is normal or elevated (unlike IDA where it is low). Malaria-related haemolytic anaemia should always be considered in endemic settings and confirmed or excluded by blood film; intravascular haemolysis produces rapidly developing severe anaemia. Sickle cell disease and sickle cell trait with complications produce haemolytic anaemia with a characteristic blood film (sickle cells, target cells). G6PD deficiency causes episodic haemolytic anaemia triggered by oxidant stress (infections, certain drugs, fava beans). Vitamin B12 or folate deficiency produces macrocytic anaemia with hypersegmented neutrophils on blood film. Thalassaemia trait produces a microcytic picture with normal or elevated ferritin; red cell count is high relative to haemoglobin.

**Features that argue against this diagnosis:** Normal or elevated MCV argues against iron deficiency as the primary cause of anaemia — consider vitamin B12 or folate deficiency (macrocytic), haemolytic causes, or anaemia of chronic disease (normocytic). Normal or elevated serum ferritin makes IDA unlikely unless significant concurrent acute-phase inflammation is present (ferritin is a positive acute-phase reactant). Absence of any identifiable source of blood loss or dietary deficiency in a man or post-menopausal woman should prompt reassessment of the diagnosis rather than empirical iron treatment — occult gastrointestinal malignancy must be excluded. Blood film showing sickle cells, target cells, or spherocytes indicates a haemolytic cause rather than IDA.

**Red flags:** Haemoglobin <7 g/dL (or <8 g/dL in symptomatic cardiovascular disease) meets WHO criteria for severe anaemia requiring urgent intervention. High-output cardiac failure — tachycardia, wide pulse pressure, systolic murmur, cardiac enlargement — complicates severe anaemia and is particularly dangerous in pregnancy. New anaemia in a man or post-menopausal woman without a clear dietary cause mandates urgent gastrointestinal investigation to exclude occult malignancy. Anaemia in pregnancy below 7 g/dL is associated with adverse maternal and fetal outcomes and requires urgent management.

**Diagnostic context:** Full blood count demonstrating low haemoglobin with low MCV (microcytosis) and low MCH (hypochromia) is the first-line investigation. Serum ferritin is the most sensitive and specific single test for iron deficiency — a level <30 μg/L is diagnostic in uncomplicated cases; ferritin may be falsely elevated in the presence of acute or chronic inflammation. Iron studies (serum iron, TIBC, transferrin saturation) complement ferritin in ambiguous cases. Blood film examination is essential in malaria-endemic settings to exclude haemolytic causes. Stool microscopy for hookworm ova should be performed in relevant epidemiological contexts. HIV testing is indicated in high-prevalence settings. A reticulocyte count after two to four weeks of oral iron therapy provides a functional test of response. The underlying cause — particularly gastrointestinal blood loss — must be identified and treated; iron supplementation alone is an incomplete response.
