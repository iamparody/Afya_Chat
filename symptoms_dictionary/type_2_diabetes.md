# Type 2 diabetes mellitus

Type 2 diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disorder characterised by hyperglycaemia resulting from progressive insulin resistance combined with relative impairment of insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells.

**Cardinal symptoms:** Polyuria results from osmotic diuresis driven by glucosuria when the renal glucose threshold is exceeded; polydipsia follows as a compensatory response to fluid loss; fatigue occurs because peripheral tissues cannot effectively utilise glucose despite elevated circulating concentrations.

**Associated symptoms and signs:** Blurred vision may occur from osmotic changes in the lens. Recurrent infections — particularly skin, urinary tract, and oral candidiasis — reflect immune impairment in a hyperglycaemic environment. Slow wound healing and peripheral neuropathy symptoms (tingling, numbness, or burning in the feet and lower limbs) indicate end-organ involvement. Acanthosis nigricans, a velvety hyperpigmented thickening at the neck, axillae, and groin, is a cutaneous marker of insulin resistance.

**Diagnostic features:** Laboratory confirmation is required. Fasting plasma glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L, two-hour OGTT glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L, HbA1c ≥48 mmol/mol (6.5%), or random plasma glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L with symptoms each meet diagnostic criteria. In asymptomatic individuals, a second confirmatory test is required. Glycosuria on urinalysis is a supportive finding. Acanthosis nigricans on examination supports insulin resistance. In established disease, peripheral neuropathy, retinopathy, or nephropathy confirm end-organ involvement but do not substitute for laboratory diagnosis.

**Predisposing factors:** Increasing age (particularly over 45 years), overweight or obesity (BMI ≥25 kg/m² or ≥23 kg/m² in Asian populations), physical inactivity, first-degree family history of type 2 diabetes, previous gestational diabetes or delivery of a large-for-gestational-age infant, impaired fasting glucose or impaired glucose tolerance, and certain ethnic backgrounds including South Asian, East African, and Middle Eastern populations carry elevated risk.

**Typical presentation:** Type 2 diabetes frequently develops over months to years and may remain entirely asymptomatic until identified through screening or an incidental finding of hyperglycaemia during investigation for another condition. When symptomatic, the classic triad of polyuria, polydipsia, and fatigue is present, often alongside an indolent history. Unlike type 1 diabetes, diabetic ketoacidosis at presentation is uncommon.

**Important differential diagnoses:** Type 1 diabetes mellitus characteristically presents in younger individuals with abrupt onset, significant weight loss, and a strong propensity for ketoacidosis; autoantibody positivity (anti-GAD, anti-IA2) and low or undetectable C-peptide support this diagnosis. Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) should be considered in lean individuals under 35 with strong family history across multiple generations and absence of typical type 2 risk factors. Diabetes insipidus produces pronounced polyuria and polydipsia but with normal blood glucose; urine is dilute and plasma osmolality elevated rather than glucose-driven. Secondary causes — glucocorticoid-induced hyperglycaemia, pancreatic disease, acromegaly, Cushing syndrome — should be excluded when clinical features do not fit straightforward type 2 diabetes.

**Features that argue against this diagnosis:** Abrupt onset with ketoacidosis in a young, lean individual argues strongly against type 2 diabetes and towards type 1 or MODY. BMI below 25 kg/m² without central adiposity in a person under 35 weakens the type 2 hypothesis. Normal fasting plasma glucose and HbA1c on two separate occasions excludes current diabetes mellitus. Absence of glycosuria argues against significant ongoing hyperglycaemia. A clear precipitating cause for hyperglycaemia — acute illness, corticosteroid use, pancreatitis — should be identified and excluded before diagnosing de novo type 2 diabetes.

**Red flags:** Severe hyperglycaemic symptoms with vomiting, dehydration, or altered mental status suggest hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state, a potentially fatal complication requiring urgent assessment. New-onset diabetic ketoacidosis should prompt reconsideration of the type 2 diagnosis. Severe acute infections, foot ulceration with systemic features, and rapidly deteriorating renal function are clinical emergencies in the context of established diabetes.

**Diagnostic context:** Diagnosis requires laboratory confirmation. Criteria (WHO 2023): fasting plasma glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L; two-hour plasma glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L during an oral glucose tolerance test; HbA1c ≥48 mmol/mol (6.5%); or random plasma glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L in the presence of hyperglycaemic symptoms. In asymptomatic individuals, two separate tests meeting diagnostic criteria are required. Point-of-care HbA1c requires standardised assay validation. Symptoms alone are insufficient for diagnosis.
