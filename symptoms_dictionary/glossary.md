# Clinical Terminology Glossary

Shared definitions for terms used consistently across condition cards. Maintained here to prevent definitional drift across files. When a term is updated here, all condition cards that use it should be reviewed for consistency.

---

**Anaemia** — Haemoglobin below WHO thresholds: <13 g/dL in men; <12 g/dL in non-pregnant women; <11 g/dL in pregnant women; <11.5 g/dL in children aged 5–11 years; <11 g/dL in children aged 6–59 months.

**Barrett's oesophagus** — Metaplastic replacement of the normal squamous epithelium of the distal oesophagus with columnar intestinal-type epithelium, confirmed on biopsy. Develops as a complication of chronic gastro-oesophageal reflux disease. Carries a small but significant risk of progression to oesophageal adenocarcinoma (approximately 0.3% per year); the absolute risk is low but the condition requires endoscopic surveillance. Risk factors for progression include segment length, male sex, obesity, and smoking.

**Globus sensation** — A persistent awareness of a lump, tightness, or foreign body in the throat, typically at the level of the cricoid cartilage, without true dysphagia (swallowing is not impaired). Causes include gastro-oesophageal reflux disease, upper oesophageal sphincter dysfunction, and anxiety. Distinguished from dysphagia (difficulty initiating swallowing or food sticking) — patients with globus can swallow normally despite the sensation.

**Lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS)** — A zone of raised pressure at the gastro-oesophageal junction, formed by the intrinsic smooth muscle of the distal oesophagus and the crural diaphragm, that acts as the primary anti-reflux barrier. Transient LOS relaxations unrelated to swallowing are the dominant mechanism of reflux in GERD. Hiatus hernia mechanically disrupts the LOS. Certain drugs (calcium channel blockers, nitrates, anticholinergics, progesterone) reduce LOS pressure and can worsen reflux.

**Odynophagia** — Pain on swallowing, felt as a sharp or burning discomfort in the chest during food or liquid passage. Distinguished from dysphagia (difficulty swallowing or food sticking without pain). Odynophagia indicates significant mucosal disease — severe oesophagitis, oesophageal ulceration, or infection (candida, CMV, HSV oesophagitis in immunocompromised patients). It is a red-flag symptom in the context of GERD and requires endoscopic evaluation.

**Waterbrash** — A sudden, involuntary flooding of the mouth with clear or slightly cloudy tasteless saliva, often occurring without warning. Caused by a vagally-mediated reflex hypersalivation response to oesophageal acid exposure. Distinguished from regurgitation (which contains sour or bitter gastric fluid) and from nausea with hypersalivation (which precedes vomiting). When present alongside heartburn, waterbrash is highly specific for gastro-oesophageal reflux disease.

**Costovertebral angle tenderness** — Pain elicited by firm percussion or palpation at the angle formed by the 12th rib and the paravertebral muscles of the lower back, bilaterally. Positive finding is unilateral and indicates ipsilateral renal parenchymal or pelvic inflammation (pyelonephritis, renal calculus).

**Dehydration (clinical grading — WHO/IMCI)** — Mild: thirst, slightly reduced urine output, normal skin turgor. Moderate: sunken eyes, reduced skin turgor (pinch returns slowly in >1 second), tachycardia, dry mucous membranes. Severe: sunken eyes, very slow skin turgor (>2 seconds), absent tears when crying, lethargy or unconsciousness, absent urine output. Severe dehydration is a medical emergency.

**Dysuria** — Pain, burning, or stinging during urination. Internal dysuria (felt inside the urethra or bladder) is more specific for urinary tract infection or urethritis. External dysuria (felt on the skin of the vulva as urine passes over it) is more specific for vaginitis or vulval irritation.

**Fever** — Core body temperature ≥38.0°C (100.4°F). Low-grade fever: 37.5–38.0°C. High fever: >39°C. Hyperpyrexia: >41°C. Method of measurement (axillary, tympanic, rectal) affects thresholds; rectal measurement is most accurate in children.

**Haematuria** — Blood in urine. Macroscopic (visible): urine appears red, pink, or brown. Microscopic: ≥3 red blood cells per high-power field on urine microscopy in the absence of contamination. Both require investigation; macroscopic haematuria without infective cause requires cystoscopy to exclude malignancy.

**Haemoptysis** — Coughing up of blood or blood-stained sputum from the lower respiratory tract. Must be distinguished from haematemesis (blood from the upper GI tract, often darker, mixed with food) and epistaxis with posterior drainage. Massive haemoptysis is defined as >200–600 mL in 24 hours and is a surgical emergency.

**Hepatosplenomegaly** — Enlargement of both the liver and the spleen on clinical examination or imaging. In East African primary care, the combination suggests malaria (particularly chronic or repeated infection), visceral leishmaniasis, schistosomiasis, haematological malignancy, or chronic viral hepatitis. Assessment: liver dullness on percussion with palpable inferior border below the costal margin; spleen confirmed by dullness in the left upper quadrant with notch on palpation.

**Jaundice** — Yellow discolouration of the skin, sclera, and mucous membranes from elevated bilirubin (>34–51 μmol/L). Pre-hepatic (haemolytic): predominantly unconjugated bilirubin, dark urine absent, pale stools absent — seen in malaria, haemolytic anaemia, sickle cell. Hepatic (parenchymal): mixed — seen in viral hepatitis, hepatotoxicity. Post-hepatic (obstructive): predominantly conjugated, dark urine, pale stools — biliary obstruction, cholangiocarcinoma.

**Koilonychia** — Spoon-shaped deformity of the fingernails where the nail plate is concave. Associated with chronic iron deficiency anaemia. May also occur normally in young children. Distinguish from nail changes of psoriasis (pitting, onycholysis) or fungal infection (discolouration, thickening).

**Left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH)** — Increase in left ventricular wall thickness or mass, typically from chronic pressure overload (hypertension, aortic stenosis). Detected on ECG by Sokolow-Lyon criteria (SV1 + RV5 or RV6 ≥35 mm) or Cornell voltage criteria (RaVL + SV3 >28 mm men, >20 mm women). Confirmed on echocardiography. Indicates target organ damage in hypertension.

**Lymphadenopathy** — Enlarged lymph nodes, defined as >1 cm in diameter for most sites (>1.5 cm inguinal, >0.5 cm epitrochlear). Localised: suggests regional infection or malignancy. Generalised (≥2 non-contiguous regions): suggests systemic infection (HIV, TB, EBV, CMV), haematological malignancy, or autoimmune disease. Characteristics suggesting malignancy: hard, fixed, non-tender, >2 cm, progressive enlargement.

**Melaena** — Black, tarry, malodorous stool from degraded blood, indicating upper gastrointestinal bleeding (proximal to the ligament of Treitz). Approximately 50–100 mL of blood in the upper GI tract is required to produce melaena. Distinguish from iron or bismuth supplementation (black stool without tarry quality or haem-positive result).

**Pallor** — Reduced colour of skin and mucous membranes from anaemia or peripheral vasoconstriction. Conjunctival pallor (inner lower eyelid) and palmar crease pallor are the most reliable clinical signs of anaemia. Peri-oral pallor and generalised pallor are less specific. Absence of conjunctival pallor has reasonable negative predictive value for severe anaemia.

**Pyuria** — White blood cells (leucocytes) in urine. Significant pyuria on microscopy: ≥10 white blood cells per mm³ in unspun urine, or ≥5 per high-power field in spun urine. Dipstick leukocyte esterase detects pyuria with approximately 75–96% sensitivity and 94–98% specificity. Sterile pyuria (pyuria without bacterial growth on culture) raises TB, STI, or interstitial nephritis.

**Rigors** — Episodes of uncontrollable shivering with teeth chattering and intense sensation of cold, typically preceding a febrile spike. Rigors indicate bacteraemia, malaria, or other systemic infection with rapid temperature change. Distinguished from simple chills (milder sensation of cold without shivering) and from febrile convulsions in children.

**Splenomegaly** — Enlarged spleen palpable below the left costal margin on deep inspiration. Graded: mild (<5 cm below costal margin), moderate (5–10 cm), massive (>10 cm). Causes in East African context: malaria (acute and chronic), sickle cell disease, visceral leishmaniasis, schistosomiasis, viral hepatitis with portal hypertension, haematological malignancy. Confirmed by percussion (dullness in left upper quadrant) and palpation.

**Tachycardia** — Resting heart rate >100 beats per minute in adults. Physiological causes: fever, pain, anxiety, dehydration. Pathological causes: sepsis, cardiac arrhythmia, pulmonary embolism, severe anaemia, thyrotoxicosis. In the context of fever, tachycardia is expected; relative bradycardia (slower heart rate than expected for the degree of fever) is a distinguishing feature of typhoid fever.

**Biphasic fever** — A fever pattern with two distinct febrile episodes separated by a brief afebrile interval of 1–3 days. The first phase corresponds to the leptospiraemic (septicaemic) phase; the second corresponds to the immune phase and may be accompanied by new or worsening symptoms. Also seen in dengue fever and some arboviral infections. A patient who reports apparent improvement followed by recurrence of fever should prompt consideration of leptospirosis.

**Conjunctival suffusion** — Intense bilateral bulbar conjunctival injection (redness) without inflammatory exudate, discharge, or photophobia. The conjunctiva appears deeply injected ("red eye") but the eye is not sticky and there is no discharge — this distinguishes suffusion from bacterial or viral conjunctivitis. Conjunctival suffusion is among the most diagnostically useful physical signs of leptospirosis and should be specifically looked for in any febrile patient with a plausible exposure history. It may co-occur with scleral icterus in icteric leptospirosis.

**LPHS (Leptospirosis-associated pulmonary haemorrhagic syndrome)** — A life-threatening complication of severe leptospirosis characterised by diffuse alveolar haemorrhage. Mediated by toxin-induced damage to the alveolar-capillary basement membrane rather than by primary inflammatory pneumonia. Patients may progress from mild respiratory symptoms to massive haemoptysis and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) within 72 hours; mortality exceeds 50%. Radiological hallmark is bilateral diffuse nodular densities ("snowflake" pattern) that may rapidly consolidate. Requires intensive care escalation immediately — do not wait for diagnostic confirmation before arranging referral.

**MAT (Microscopic Agglutination Test)** — The serological gold standard for confirming leptospirosis. Live or killed Leptospira antigens from multiple serovars are incubated with serial dilutions of patient serum; agglutination is read under dark-field microscopy. A single titre of ≥1:800 or a fourfold rise between paired acute and convalescent sera is confirmatory. MAT is retrospective (peak titres appear 2–4 weeks after onset), serovar-specific, technically demanding, and typically available only at reference laboratories. IgM ELISA and lateral flow assays are used as accessible field screening tools; a positive result should be confirmed by MAT where possible.

**Weil disease** — Severe icteric leptospirosis characterised by the triad of jaundice, acute kidney injury, and haemorrhagic manifestations (including thrombocytopenia, mucosal bleeding, and pulmonary haemorrhage). Caused by leptospiral infection of the liver (disruption of hepatocellular junctions) and kidney (acute interstitial nephritis and tubular necrosis). Jaundice in Weil disease is disproportionately deep relative to the degree of transaminase elevation — a pattern that distinguishes it from viral hepatitis. Mortality is substantially higher than anicteric leptospirosis. Immediate referral to a facility with renal support capacity is required at first suspicion.

**FEV1/FVC ratio** — The ratio of Forced Expiratory Volume in 1 second (FEV1) to Forced Vital Capacity (FVC), measured by spirometry. A post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC ratio below 0.70 confirms persistent airflow limitation and is the GOLD-standard spirometric criterion for diagnosing COPD. In asthma, this ratio typically normalises (or substantially improves) after bronchodilator administration — its persistence after bronchodilator administration distinguishes COPD from asthma. Spirometry is usually unavailable at Kenya primary care level; a clinical diagnosis is made from history and examination, with definitive confirmation referred to a higher facility.

**Tachypnoea** — Respiratory rate >20 breaths per minute in adults. In children: >50 in infants aged 2–12 months; >40 in children aged 12 months to 5 years; >30 in children over 5 years. Tachypnoea is a reliable early sign of lower respiratory tract infection, metabolic acidosis, pulmonary embolism, and severe dehydration. It is one of the most sensitive early indicators of deterioration in hospitalised patients.

**Bacteriuria** — Bacteria present in urine. Significant bacteriuria is conventionally defined as ≥10⁵ colony-forming units per mL on a mid-stream urine culture, though lower counts may be significant in symptomatic women or in specimens obtained by catheterisation. Asymptomatic bacteriuria (significant bacteriuria without urinary symptoms) is common in older adults and in patients with indwelling catheters, and is generally **not** treated — the exceptions are pregnancy and before urological procedures. Treating asymptomatic bacteriuria otherwise drives antimicrobial resistance without clinical benefit.

**Complicated urinary tract infection** — A urinary tract infection occurring in the presence of a structural or functional abnormality of the urinary tract, or of a host comorbidity that increases the risk of treatment failure. Recognised complicating factors include pregnancy, diabetes mellitus, immunocompromise, kidney transplant, urinary catheter, urinary stones, renal failure, and hospital-acquired infection. **Urinary tract infection in a male is considered complicated by definition.** The distinction matters at primary-care level because complicated infection changes the referral threshold rather than only the antibiotic choice.

**Cystitis** — Infection and inflammation confined to the bladder and associated structures; synonymous with lower urinary tract infection. Presents with dysuria, urinary frequency, urgency, and suprapubic discomfort. Fever, loin pain, costovertebral angle tenderness, nausea or vomiting indicate that infection has ascended to the upper tract and the presentation should be reassessed as pyelonephritis rather than cystitis.

**Mid-stream urine (MSU)** — A urine specimen collected after the initial stream has been discarded, so that periurethral and distal-urethral flora are flushed away before collection. Reduces contamination and is the standard specimen for urine culture. A contaminated specimen (mixed growth, epithelial cells on microscopy) cannot be interpreted and should be repeated rather than treated.

**Nitrite (urinary dipstick)** — Dietary nitrate reduced to nitrite by bacterial nitrate reductase. A positive dipstick nitrite is highly specific for urinary tract infection (approximately 95–98%) but poorly sensitive. Two common causes of a false negative: the organism does not produce nitrate reductase — *Enterococcus*, *Staphylococcus saprophyticus*, *Pseudomonas* — and insufficient bladder dwell time, since conversion requires urine to remain in the bladder for roughly four hours. **A negative nitrite does not exclude urinary tract infection.**

**Digital rectal examination (DRE)** — Palpation of the prostate through the anterior rectal wall with a gloved lubricated finger, assessing size, consistency, symmetry and tenderness. In acute bacterial prostatitis the prostate is characteristically soft, swollen, tense, and severely tender to light pressure. ⛔ **Prostate massage must not be performed when acute bacterial prostatitis is suspected** — it is intensely painful and can force bacteria into the bloodstream, precipitating bacteraemia and sepsis. Gentle palpation to establish tenderness is sufficient; vigorous or repeated examination adds risk without adding information.

**Prostatitis categories** — Prostatitis is classified into four categories: **I** acute bacterial prostatitis; **II** chronic bacterial prostatitis; **III** chronic prostatitis / chronic pelvic pain syndrome, subdivided into inflammatory (IIIa) and non-inflammatory (IIIb); **IV** asymptomatic inflammatory prostatitis. Only category I is an acute febrile illness requiring urgent recognition and referral. Categories II–IV are chronic presentations managed on a different pathway, and conflating them with category I risks under-treating a septic patient.

**Acute urinary retention** — Sudden inability to pass urine despite a full bladder, typically with suprapubic pain and a palpable distended bladder. A urological emergency requiring prompt catheterisation and referral. In the context of suspected acute bacterial prostatitis it indicates prostatic swelling obstructing the urethra. Distinguish from anuria, in which no urine is being produced and the bladder is empty — the two have opposite causes and opposite management.

**Proteinuria** — Protein detectable in urine. A dipstick result of ≥1+ warrants quantification and repeat testing. Transient proteinuria may follow fever, exercise, or upright posture and does not indicate renal disease. Persistent proteinuria indicates glomerular or tubulointerstitial pathology and requires investigation. Nephrotic-range proteinuria in adults is ≥3.5 g per 24 hours and, together with hypoalbuminaemia, is an indispensable prerequisite for the diagnosis of nephrotic syndrome. Proteinuria accompanying pyuria and haematuria suggests parenchymal renal disease rather than simple urinary tract infection.
