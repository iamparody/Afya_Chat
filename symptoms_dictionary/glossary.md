# Clinical Terminology Glossary

Shared definitions for terms used consistently across condition cards. Maintained here to prevent definitional drift across files. When a term is updated here, all condition cards that use it should be reviewed for consistency.

---

**Anaemia** — Haemoglobin below WHO thresholds: <13 g/dL in men; <12 g/dL in non-pregnant women; <11 g/dL in pregnant women; <11.5 g/dL in children aged 5–11 years; <11 g/dL in children aged 6–59 months.

**Costovertebral angle tenderness** — Pain elicited by firm percussion or palpation at the angle formed by the 12th rib and the paravertebral muscles of the lower back, bilaterally. Positive finding is unilateral and indicates ipsilateral renal parenchymal or pelvic inflammation (pyelonephritis, renal calculus).

**Dehydration (clinical grading — WHO/IMCI)** — Mild: thirst, slightly reduced urine output, normal skin turgor. Moderate: sunken eyes, reduced skin turgor (pinch returns slowly in >1 second), tachycardia, dry mucous membranes. Severe: sunken eyes, very slow skin turgor (>2 seconds), absent tears when crying, lethargy or unconsciousness, absent urine output. Severe dehydration is a medical emergency.

**Dysuria** — Pain, burning, or stinging during urination. Internal dysuria (felt inside the urethra or bladder) is more specific for urinary tract infection or urethritis. External dysuria (felt on the skin of the vulva as urine passes over it) is more specific for vaginitis or vulval irritation.

**Fever** — Core body temperature ≥38.0°C (100.4°F). Low-grade fever: 37.5–38.0°C. High fever: >39°C. Hyperpyrexia: >41°C. Method of measurement (axillary, tympanic, rectal) affects thresholds; rectal measurement is most accurate in children.

**Haematuria** — Blood in urine. Macroscopic (visible): urine appears red, pink, or brown. Microscopic: ≥3 red blood cells per high-power field on urine microscopy in the absence of contamination. Both require investigation; macroscopic haematuria without infective cause requires cystoscopy to exclude malignancy.

**Haemoptysis** — Coughing up of blood or blood-stained sputum from the lower respiratory tract. Must be distinguished from haematemesis (blood from the upper GI tract, often darker, mixed with food) and epistaxis with posterior drainage. Massive haemoptysis is defined as >200–600 mL in 24 hours and is a surgical emergency.

**Hepatosplenomegaly** — Enlargement of both the liver and the spleen on clinical examination or imaging. In East African primary care, the combination suggests malaria (particularly chronic or repeated infection), visceral leishmaniasis, schistosomiasis, haematological malignancy, or chronic viral hepatitis. Assessment: liver dullness on percussion with palpable inferior border below the costal margin; spleen confirmed by dullness in the left upper quadrant with notch on palpation.

**Jaundice** — Yellow discolouration of the skin, sclera, and mucous membranes from elevated bilirubin (>34–51 μmol/L). Pre-hepatic (haemolytic): predominantly unconjugated bilirubin, dark urine absent, pale stools absent — seen in malaria, haemolytic anaemia, sickle cell. Hepatic (parenchymal): mixed — seen in viral hepatitis, hepatotoxicity. Post-hepatic (obstructive): predominantly conjugated, dark urine, pale stools — biliary obstruction, cholangiocarcinoma.

**Koilonychia** — Spoon-shaped deformity of the fingernails where the nail plate is concave. Associated with chronic iron deficiency anaemia. May also occur normally in young children. Distinguish from nail changes of psoriasis (pitting, onycholysis) or fungal infection (discolouration, thickening).

**Left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH)** — Increase in left ventricular wall thickness or mass, typically from chronic pressure overload (hypertension, aortic stenosis). Detected on ECG by Sokolow-Lyon criteria (SV1 + RV5 or RV6 ≥35 mm) or Cornell voltage criteria (RaVL + SV3 >28 mm men, >20 mm women). Confirmed on echocardiography. Indicates target organ damage in hypertension.

**Lymphadenopathy** — Enlarged lymph nodes, defined as >1 cm in diameter for most sites (>1.5 cm inguinal, >0.5 cm epitrochlear). Localised: suggests regional infection or malignancy. Generalised (≥2 non-contiguous regions): suggests systemic infection (HIV, TB, EBV, CMV), haematological malignancy, or autoimmune disease. Characteristics suggesting malignancy: hard, fixed, non-tender, >2 cm, progressive enlargement.

**Melaena** — Black, tarry, malodorous stool from degraded blood, indicating upper gastrointestinal bleeding (proximal to the ligament of Treitz). Approximately 50–100 mL of blood in the upper GI tract is required to produce melaena. Distinguish from iron or bismuth supplementation (black stool without tarry quality or haem-positive result).

**Pallor** — Reduced colour of skin and mucous membranes from anaemia or peripheral vasoconstriction. Conjunctival pallor (inner lower eyelid) and palmar crease pallor are the most reliable clinical signs of anaemia. Peri-oral pallor and generalised pallor are less specific. Absence of conjunctival pallor has reasonable negative predictive value for severe anaemia.

**Pyuria** — White blood cells (leucocytes) in urine. Significant pyuria on microscopy: ≥10 white blood cells per mm³ in unspun urine, or ≥5 per high-power field in spun urine. Dipstick leukocyte esterase detects pyuria with approximately 75–96% sensitivity and 94–98% specificity. Sterile pyuria (pyuria without bacterial growth on culture) raises TB, STI, or interstitial nephritis.

**Rigors** — Episodes of uncontrollable shivering with teeth chattering and intense sensation of cold, typically preceding a febrile spike. Rigors indicate bacteraemia, malaria, or other systemic infection with rapid temperature change. Distinguished from simple chills (milder sensation of cold without shivering) and from febrile convulsions in children.

**Splenomegaly** — Enlarged spleen palpable below the left costal margin on deep inspiration. Graded: mild (<5 cm below costal margin), moderate (5–10 cm), massive (>10 cm). Causes in East African context: malaria (acute and chronic), sickle cell disease, visceral leishmaniasis, schistosomiasis, viral hepatitis with portal hypertension, haematological malignancy. Confirmed by percussion (dullness in left upper quadrant) and palpation.

**Tachycardia** — Resting heart rate >100 beats per minute in adults. Physiological causes: fever, pain, anxiety, dehydration. Pathological causes: sepsis, cardiac arrhythmia, pulmonary embolism, severe anaemia, thyrotoxicosis. In the context of fever, tachycardia is expected; relative bradycardia (slower heart rate than expected for the degree of fever) is a distinguishing feature of typhoid fever.

**Tachypnoea** — Respiratory rate >20 breaths per minute in adults. In children: >50 in infants aged 2–12 months; >40 in children aged 12 months to 5 years; >30 in children over 5 years. Tachypnoea is a reliable early sign of lower respiratory tract infection, metabolic acidosis, pulmonary embolism, and severe dehydration. It is one of the most sensitive early indicators of deterioration in hospitalised patients.
