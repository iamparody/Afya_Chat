# Malaria

Malaria is a life-threatening parasitic infection transmitted by the bite of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes, caused in humans predominantly by Plasmodium falciparum (the most dangerous species, responsible for severe malaria), P. vivax, P. ovale, P. malariae, and P. knowlesi.

**Cardinal symptoms:** Fever is the cardinal feature, often with an accompanying history of chills and rigors. The classic periodicity — tertian fever every 48 hours in P. vivax/P. ovale, quartan every 72 hours in P. malariae — is frequently absent in clinical practice, particularly in falciparum malaria where fever is commonly continuous or irregular. Headache and malaise accompany virtually all presentations.

**Associated symptoms and signs:** Myalgia and arthralgia are prominent and may resemble influenza. Nausea, vomiting, and anorexia are common. Profuse diaphoresis follows the febrile paroxysm. Splenomegaly occurs with repeated or chronic infection and may be marked. Anaemia develops from haemolysis of infected and uninfected red cells. Jaundice reflects haemolysis and hepatic involvement. In children, pallor, irritability, and poor feeding may predominate over the classic febrile picture.

**Diagnostic features:** Positive malaria rapid diagnostic test (RDT) detecting parasite antigens is the recommended first-line confirmatory test in peripheral settings. Positive thick blood film demonstrating malaria parasites allows species identification and parasite density quantification — this is the gold standard and should be performed alongside RDT wherever possible. Positive thin blood film confirms species and identifies P. falciparum ring forms or P. vivax enlarged red cells. Positive PCR confirms low-density parasitaemia not detectable by microscopy. Temporal correlation with exposure in an endemic area within the expected incubation period (7–30 days for P. falciparum, up to 12 months for P. vivax) is required context but is not diagnostic alone.

**Predisposing factors:** Residence in or travel to a malaria-endemic region is the necessary condition for exposure. Within endemic areas, risk is elevated in children under five years (particularly for severe malaria and death), pregnant women (especially primigravidae, due to placental malaria), individuals without acquired immunity (including migrants and travellers from non-endemic areas), HIV infection, and sickle cell disease heterozygotes have partial protection while homozygotes have increased severity. Lack of insecticide-treated bed net use, indoor residual spraying gaps, and inadequate vector control are population-level risk factors.

**Typical presentation:** In endemic settings, malaria in a child commonly presents with fever, pallor, and irritability, with rapid progression to severe disease possible in falciparum infection. In adults with partial immunity, the presentation is often less dramatic — low-grade fever, fatigue, and headache over several days. In non-immune adults or travellers, onset is acute within 7–30 days of exposure (longer incubation possible with P. vivax and P. malariae), with high fever, rigors, and systemic toxicity.

**Important differential diagnoses:** Typhoid fever (enteric fever) may produce a similar febrile illness but characteristically presents with insidious onset, relative bradycardia for the degree of fever, abdominal pain, constipation or diarrhoea, rose-coloured spots on the trunk, and leukopenia; Widal test or blood culture distinguishes it. Dengue fever is characterised by severe myalgia and bone pain ("break-bone fever"), retro-orbital headache, maculopapular rash, and thrombocytopenia; no rigors and haemorrhagic features in severe cases. Influenza produces fever, myalgia, and headache without the periodicity or splenomegaly of malaria. Bacterial sepsis may closely mimic severe falciparum malaria; concurrent bacteraemia is possible. Leptospirosis in endemic areas presents with fever, headache, myalgia, and conjunctival suffusion, progressing to jaundice and renal failure in Weil disease. Meningitis must be considered when headache and fever are accompanied by neck stiffness or altered consciousness, even if malaria is confirmed.

**Features that argue against this diagnosis:** Complete absence of fever — current or recent — makes malaria unlikely in a non-immune individual presenting acutely. Negative RDT and negative blood films on two separate examinations 12–24 hours apart substantially reduces the probability of malaria; a third sample is indicated only if clinical suspicion remains high. Symptom onset more than 30 days after departing an endemic area makes P. falciparum malaria less likely (though P. vivax and P. ovale can relapse months to over a year later). No history of exposure to a malaria-endemic area or to an Anopheles mosquito-dense environment effectively excludes locally acquired malaria in a non-endemic setting.

**Red flags (severe malaria — Plasmodium falciparum):** Altered consciousness or coma (cerebral malaria) is the most dangerous feature. Severe anaemia (haemoglobin <7 g/dL in children, <8 g/dL in adults) requiring transfusion. Respiratory distress — indicating pulmonary oedema or metabolic acidosis. Hypoglycaemia (glucose <2.2 mmol/L), particularly in children and pregnant women. Haemoglobinuria (blackwater fever). Seizures. Inability to sit or stand, prostration. Vomiting without the ability to retain oral medications. Any of these features requires immediate parenteral artesunate treatment and intensive monitoring.

**Diagnostic context:** Clinical diagnosis alone is insufficient and should never be the basis for treatment decisions where testing is available. Rapid diagnostic tests (malaria RDTs) detect parasite antigens and are the recommended first-line test in peripheral settings. Thick and thin blood films allow species identification, parasite density quantification, and detection of gametocytes; this remains the gold standard and should be performed alongside RDT wherever possible. Negative RDT in a highly suspected case should prompt repeat testing and clinical escalation. Species identification is critical — P. falciparum requires artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) and urgent assessment for severity.
