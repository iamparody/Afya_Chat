# Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease

Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is a condition in which the retrograde flow of gastric contents into the oesophagus produces troublesome symptoms or mucosal complications. It results from dysfunction of the lower oesophageal sphincter — transient relaxations, reduced resting pressure, or mechanical disruption from a hiatus hernia — allowing acidic gastric content to contact the oesophageal mucosa repeatedly.

**Cardinal symptoms:** Heartburn — a burning sensation rising from the epigastrium towards the throat — is the hallmark, typically occurring after meals, on lying down, or on bending forward. Acid regurgitation — the effortless return of sour or bitter gastric fluid into the throat or mouth — is the second cardinal feature and distinguishes GERD from functional dyspepsia. Waterbrash — a sudden flooding of the mouth with clear saliva — represents a vagal reflex response to oesophageal acidification and is highly specific for GERD when present.

**Associated symptoms and signs:** Nausea and epigastric pain are common. Bloating and belching frequently accompany meals. Odynophagia — pain on swallowing — indicates significant oesophagitis or oesophageal ulceration and warrants investigation. Extra-oesophageal manifestations are clinically important: hoarseness from posterior laryngeal acid exposure (laryngopharyngeal reflux), globus sensation (a persistent awareness of a lump or tightness in the throat without true dysphagia), and chronic cough — typically dry, nocturnal, and unresponsive to antitussives — all reflect acid injury beyond the oesophagus. Chest pain mimicking cardiac pain may occur from oesophageal spasm triggered by reflux and can be difficult to distinguish from ischaemic pain without investigation.

**Diagnostic features:** GERD is primarily a clinical diagnosis when both cardinal symptoms are present without alarm features. A therapeutic trial of a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) — omeprazole 20–40 mg once daily for 4–8 weeks — is diagnostic and therapeutic simultaneously; symptom resolution confirms the diagnosis in the primary care setting. Upper GI endoscopy is required for alarm features, failure of PPI therapy, or monitoring of established Barrett's oesophagus; it may reveal oesophagitis, erosions, ulceration, or Barrett's metaplasia, or may be normal (non-erosive reflux disease — the commonest endoscopic finding). Twenty-four-hour ambulatory oesophageal pH monitoring or pH-impedance testing is the gold standard for confirming pathological acid exposure; it is reserved for refractory or atypical cases and pre-surgical evaluation.

**Predisposing factors:** Obesity — particularly central adiposity — is the strongest modifiable risk factor, increasing intra-abdominal pressure and promoting reflux. Pregnancy raises intra-abdominal pressure and progesterone reduces lower oesophageal sphincter tone, making GERD almost universal in the third trimester. Hiatus hernia — herniation of part of the stomach through the diaphragmatic hiatus — disrupts the anti-reflux barrier mechanically and significantly worsens reflux. Smoking impairs oesophageal mucosal defence and reduces lower oesophageal sphincter pressure. Excessive alcohol directly irritates the oesophageal mucosa. NSAID and aspirin use impair mucosal defence. Dietary precipitants — large meals, fatty and spicy foods, caffeine, chocolate — and postural factors (lying within two to three hours of eating) worsen symptoms.

**Typical presentation:** A middle-aged adult with central obesity reports a burning retrosternal and epigastric discomfort after large meals, worse at night and on lying flat, partially relieved by sitting upright or antacids. Sour regurgitation is reported especially on bending or on waking. Symptoms have been present for months to years, waxing and waning, and are often worse after fatty or spicy meals or alcohol. The patient is generally well between episodes without systemic features.

**Important differential diagnoses:** Peptic ulcer disease produces epigastric burning or gnawing with a characteristic food-timing relationship — duodenal ulcer pain relieved by food, gastric ulcer pain worsened by food — and the predominant symptom location is epigastric rather than retrosternal; H. pylori testing and endoscopy differentiate. Ischaemic heart disease can produce a retrosternal burning or pressure sensation closely mimicking GERD; a cardiac cause must be actively excluded in all patients over 40 with chest discomfort, particularly if exertional, radiating to the arm or jaw, or associated with dyspnoea; ECG and troponin are required before attributing chest pain to GERD. Functional dyspepsia produces epigastric discomfort related to meals but without the predominant heartburn and acid regurgitation characteristic of GERD; endoscopy is required to distinguish functional dyspepsia from peptic ulcer disease and erosive oesophagitis. Oesophageal spasm causes severe episodic retrosternal chest pain, often indistinguishable from cardiac pain, with normal coronary angiography; it may coexist with GERD. Eosinophilic oesophagitis presents with dysphagia and food bolus obstruction predominantly in young men; biopsies from endoscopy showing eosinophilic infiltration are required for diagnosis.

**Features that argue against this diagnosis:** Complete absence of heartburn and acid regurgitation makes GERD substantially less likely as the primary explanation for chest or upper abdominal symptoms. Symptoms that are clearly exertional — occurring during physical activity and relieved by rest or nitrates — point strongly towards ischaemic heart disease and require cardiac evaluation before GERD is considered. Prominent diarrhoea, bloody stool, or symptoms without any postprandial or positional component are more consistent with peptic ulcer disease, inflammatory bowel disease, or infective gastroenteritis than with GERD. Dysphagia as the dominant symptom — particularly to solids, progressing over weeks — without significant heartburn or regurgitation is more consistent with a structural oesophageal lesion (stricture, malignancy) and requires urgent endoscopy rather than an empirical PPI trial.

**Red flags:** Progressive dysphagia — to solids, worsening over weeks — requires urgent endoscopy to exclude oesophageal cancer or severe stricture. Odynophagia indicates significant mucosal disease and must be investigated. Unintentional weight loss accompanying reflux symptoms raises concern for oesophageal or gastric malignancy and mandates early endoscopy. Haematemesis from oesophageal or gastric bleeding requires urgent endoscopy and resuscitation. New-onset dyspepsia or heartburn in a patient over 55 without prior GERD diagnosis warrants early endoscopy to exclude Barrett's oesophagus and early oesophageal malignancy. Unexplained anaemia — iron deficiency from occult oesophageal or gastric bleeding — requires endoscopy.

**Diagnostic context:** In primary care in East Africa, GERD is managed empirically without endoscopy when classical symptoms are present and alarm features are absent. A therapeutic PPI trial is the standard approach: symptom resolution on PPI with relapse on stopping supports the diagnosis; failure to respond after four weeks requires reassessment and consideration of alternative diagnoses. H. pylori testing (urea breath test or stool antigen) should be performed before starting long-term PPI therapy — eradication may improve GERD symptoms in co-infected patients and reduces the risk of atrophic gastritis with prolonged acid suppression. Lifestyle modification should be offered alongside pharmacological management: weight reduction, elevation of the head of the bed by 15–20 cm, avoidance of meals within two to three hours of lying down, smoking cessation, and moderation of alcohol, caffeine, and fatty foods. Antacids provide symptomatic relief but not mucosal healing. Endoscopy is indicated for alarm features, failure of optimised PPI therapy, screening for Barrett's oesophagus in patients with chronic GERD and obesity, or diagnostic uncertainty.
