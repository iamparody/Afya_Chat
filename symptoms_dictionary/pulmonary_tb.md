# Pulmonary tuberculosis

Pulmonary tuberculosis is an airborne infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, characterised by granulomatous inflammation in the lung parenchyma, and remains one of the leading infectious causes of morbidity and mortality globally, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia.

**Cardinal symptoms:** Productive cough persisting for two or more weeks is the cardinal feature; sputum may be mucoid, purulent, or blood-streaked. Night sweats are characteristically drenching and often the first symptom to prompt consultation. Constitutional weight loss — often profound — and fatigue accompany most symptomatic cases. Low-grade fever, typically in the afternoon, is common.

**Associated symptoms and signs:** Haemoptysis, ranging from blood-streaked sputum to frank haemorrhage, occurs with cavitary disease or endobronchial involvement. Pleuritic chest pain may reflect pleural involvement. Dyspnoea suggests extensive parenchymal disease, pleural effusion, or pneumothorax. Lymphadenopathy — particularly cervical — indicates lymph node involvement which may occur alongside pulmonary disease. On examination, signs of consolidation or cavitation may be present; the physical examination may be surprisingly unremarkable even in extensive radiological disease.

**Diagnostic features:** Positive GeneXpert MTB/RIF (Xpert Ultra) on sputum simultaneously detects M. tuberculosis and rifampicin resistance within two hours — WHO-recommended first-line test in high-burden settings. Positive acid-fast bacilli (AFB) smear on Ziehl-Neelsen or auramine-stained sputum is widely available but less sensitive. Positive mycobacterial culture on liquid or solid media is the gold standard for definitive diagnosis and drug susceptibility testing. Characteristic chest X-ray findings — upper lobe infiltrates with or without cavitation, fibrotic changes — are supportive but not diagnostic; appearances are highly variable in HIV co-infection. Positive tuberculin skin test (TST) or IGRA indicates M. tuberculosis infection but does not distinguish latent from active disease.

**Predisposing factors:** HIV co-infection is the single greatest risk factor for progression from latent to active tuberculosis, and for severe or atypical disease. Close household contact with a smear-positive TB case. Malnutrition and underweight (BMI <18.5 kg/m²). Diabetes mellitus approximately triples TB risk. Smoking, excessive alcohol use, and overcrowded living conditions are additional modifiable risks. Silicosis and chronic renal failure are medical risk factors. Previous TB treatment increases the risk of relapse and drug resistance.

**Typical presentation:** Pulmonary TB has an insidious onset developing over weeks to months. A patient who presents as having had "a persistent cough and weight loss" for six to eight weeks before seeking care is a classic history. Night sweats and progressive weight loss in a young adult in an endemic setting should be presumed TB until proven otherwise. HIV-positive individuals may present atypically — with less or no cough, lower-lobe or miliary infiltrates on chest X-ray, and disseminated disease — and the threshold for investigation should be lower.

**Important differential diagnoses:** Community-acquired pneumonia presents with acute-onset fever, cough, and consolidation, typically responds to a course of broad-spectrum antibiotics within days to weeks, and lacks the constitutional weight loss and chronicity of TB. Lung cancer — particularly in older adults or smokers — may produce haemoptysis, weight loss, and a chest mass; chest CT and bronchoscopy are required to distinguish. Bronchiectasis produces chronic productive cough with large volumes of purulent sputum but without night sweats and with a longer history; prior TB is itself a cause. Non-tuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) infection may produce identical clinical and radiological features in immunocompromised individuals and requires culture with speciation to distinguish. Lymphoma in the mediastinum may present with night sweats, weight loss, and cough. Fungal infections (histoplasmosis, aspergillosis) may mimic TB, particularly in HIV-positive patients.

**Features that argue against this diagnosis:** Acute illness developing over fewer than seven days without weight loss or night sweats argues against pulmonary TB, which characteristically has an insidious course over weeks to months. Complete and rapid resolution of all symptoms with a standard course of broad-spectrum antibiotics makes active TB less likely, though partial overlap with bacterial pneumonia is possible. Two consecutive high-quality sputum samples negative on both smear microscopy and GeneXpert substantially reduces the probability of smear-positive and GeneXpert-detectable TB; paucibacillary TB in immunocompromised individuals cannot be excluded on this basis alone. Normal chest X-ray in an immunocompetent adult with a short illness duration argues against established pulmonary TB.

**Red flags:** Features suggesting multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB): prior TB treatment (especially incomplete treatment), known contact with a confirmed MDR-TB case, treatment failure or relapse. Clinical deterioration despite standard first-line treatment warrants urgent drug susceptibility testing. Haemoptysis exceeding 200 mL in 24 hours is a surgical emergency. HIV-positive patients with advanced immunosuppression (CD4 <200 cells/μL) presenting with suspected TB are at high risk for disseminated or CNS disease and require urgent assessment. Miliary TB — haematogenous dissemination with characteristic chest X-ray appearance and meningeal, hepatic, and splenic involvement — is a medical emergency with high mortality.

**Diagnostic context:** Sputum microbiological confirmation is the standard. WHO recommends GeneXpert MTB/RIF (Xpert Ultra) as the initial diagnostic test in high-burden settings — it simultaneously detects M. tuberculosis and rifampicin resistance within two hours. Two sputum samples (spot and early morning) should be submitted. Smear microscopy using Ziehl-Neelsen or auramine staining is less sensitive but widely available. Culture on liquid or solid media is the gold standard for definitive diagnosis and drug susceptibility testing. Chest X-ray is a supportive tool — upper lobe infiltrates with or without cavitation are the classic findings, but appearances are highly variable especially in HIV co-infection. HIV testing should be offered to all patients diagnosed with TB. Treatment must follow national and WHO guidelines with directly observed therapy for drug-susceptible TB.
