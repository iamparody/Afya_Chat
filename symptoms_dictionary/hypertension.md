# Essential hypertension

Essential (primary) hypertension is a sustained elevation in systemic arterial blood pressure — defined as ≥140 mmHg systolic and/or ≥90 mmHg diastolic — without an identifiable secondary cause, and is the leading modifiable cardiovascular risk factor globally.

**Cardinal symptoms:** Essential hypertension is predominantly asymptomatic throughout most of its course, earning the designation "the silent killer." Blood pressure elevation itself does not reliably produce symptoms until it reaches severe levels or until end-organ damage has occurred.

**Associated symptoms and signs:** When symptoms do occur, occipital headache upon waking is the most commonly reported, though its specificity for hypertension is limited. Visual disturbance, palpitations, chest pain, epistaxis, and dyspnoea are reported in severe or end-organ-complicated hypertension. On examination, fundoscopic changes (arteriovenous nicking, haemorrhages, exudates, papilloedema), a left ventricular heave, and a fourth heart sound may indicate established target-organ involvement.

**Diagnostic features:** Confirmed blood pressure ≥140 mmHg systolic and/or ≥90 mmHg diastolic on at least two separate occasions using validated technique (patient rested ≥5 minutes, appropriate cuff size, two readings per visit, averaged). Mean daytime ambulatory blood pressure ≥135/85 mmHg on 24-hour ABPM is the reference standard for confirmation. ECG demonstrating left ventricular hypertrophy by Sokolow-Lyon (SV1 + RV5/V6 ≥35 mm) or Cornell voltage criteria indicates target organ damage. Elevated urine albumin:creatinine ratio confirms early renal involvement. Fundoscopic changes (arteriovenous nicking, haemorrhages, papilloedema) confirm hypertensive retinopathy.

**Predisposing factors:** Non-modifiable factors include advancing age, male sex (pre-menopausal females have lower risk), and family history. Modifiable factors include obesity, high dietary sodium intake, physical inactivity, excessive alcohol consumption, and smoking. Secondary contributors — diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and obstructive sleep apnoea — amplify risk. In sub-Saharan Africa, hypertension is particularly prevalent and frequently undetected.

**Typical presentation:** Most patients are identified incidentally during a health visit for an unrelated complaint or through community screening. A substantial proportion have never had their blood pressure measured. When symptomatic, presentations are frequently those of complications — stroke, myocardial infarction, heart failure, or renal impairment — rather than hypertension itself.

**Important differential diagnoses:** White coat hypertension (elevated clinic measurements with normal ambulatory readings) should be distinguished using 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring, particularly before committing a patient to long-term therapy. Secondary hypertension must be considered when hypertension presents before age 30 without typical risk factors, is resistant to three or more agents, or is accompanied by suggestive features: hypokalaemia and alkalosis suggest primary hyperaldosteronism; episodic headache, palpitations, and diaphoresis suggest phaeochromocytoma; abdominal bruit or asymmetric renal size suggests renovascular hypertension; moon face, striae, and truncal obesity suggest Cushing syndrome; delayed femoral pulses suggest aortic coarctation. Pre-eclampsia must be excluded in any pregnant or recently postpartum woman with new hypertension.

**Features that argue against this diagnosis:** A single elevated reading without repeat confirmation is not diagnostic — white coat effect and situational elevation are common. Consistently normal readings on repeated home or ambulatory monitoring in a patient with elevated clinic readings confirms white coat hypertension rather than sustained hypertension. Elevated blood pressure in the clear context of acute pain, extreme anxiety, urinary retention, or sympathomimetic drug use should not be labelled as hypertension without repeat assessment in a neutral state. Normal clinic and ambulatory readings across multiple occasions reliably exclude sustained hypertension.

**Red flags:** Systolic ≥180 mmHg and/or diastolic ≥120 mmHg constitutes a hypertensive emergency when accompanied by acute end-organ damage — encephalopathy, retinal haemorrhage, papilloedema, acute coronary syndrome, aortic dissection, acute pulmonary oedema, or acute kidney injury. Sudden severe headache with neurological signs demands urgent exclusion of haemorrhagic stroke. New hypertension in pregnancy beyond 20 weeks gestation requires immediate assessment for pre-eclampsia.

**Diagnostic context:** Diagnosis requires confirmation of elevated blood pressure on at least two separate occasions using correct technique (patient rested, appropriate cuff size, two measurements per visit, averaged). Ambulatory blood pressure monitoring is the reference standard when white coat hypertension or masked hypertension is suspected. Initial workup includes urinalysis, serum electrolytes and creatinine, fasting glucose and lipids, and an ECG to detect left ventricular hypertrophy. Risk stratification using a validated cardiovascular risk score (e.g. WHO CVD Risk Charts) should guide intensity of management.
