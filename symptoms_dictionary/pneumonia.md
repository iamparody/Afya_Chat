# Community-acquired pneumonia

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is an acute infection of the lower respiratory tract — lung parenchyma — acquired outside a healthcare setting, characterised by new pulmonary infiltrate on imaging and at least one of fever, cough, purulent sputum, pleuritic chest pain, or evidence of consolidation on examination.

**Cardinal symptoms:** Productive cough with purulent sputum — yellow, green, or rust-coloured — is the most consistent feature. Fever with rigors indicates systemic infection. Pleuritic chest pain — sharp, unilateral, worsening with inspiration and cough — localises to the area of pleural involvement. Dyspnoea in proportion to the extent of disease is present in moderate to severe cases.

**Associated symptoms and signs:** Systemic features include fatigue, myalgia, and anorexia. Tachycardia and tachypnoea are reliable signs of severity. Examination of the chest may reveal localised dullness to percussion, bronchial breathing, and coarse inspiratory crackles over the affected lobe. Reduced oxygen saturation on pulse oximetry is an objective severity marker. Herpes labialis may accompany pneumococcal pneumonia. In elderly patients, confusion may be the dominant or initial presenting feature even before respiratory symptoms are prominent.

**Diagnostic features:** Chest X-ray demonstrating new pulmonary infiltrate — consolidation, lobar opacification, or ground-glass opacity — in the appropriate clinical context is the standard confirmatory investigation. Fever ≥38°C with productive cough and localising chest signs (dullness to percussion, bronchial breathing, crackles) form the clinical diagnostic triad. Elevated white cell count with neutrophilia supports bacterial aetiology. Oxygen saturation below 94% on room air is an objective severity marker. Positive sputum gram stain and culture identify the causative organism. Positive pneumococcal urinary antigen confirms S. pneumoniae pneumonia with high specificity.

**Predisposing factors:** Age extremes (under five and over 65 years) carry the highest risk. HIV infection and other immunocompromised states (diabetes, malignancy, corticosteroid use, chemotherapy) significantly elevate risk and modify the likely causative organism. Smoking damages mucociliary clearance. Chronic lung disease (asthma, COPD, bronchiectasis). Malnutrition, overcrowding, and indoor air pollution from biomass fuel combustion are important risk factors in sub-Saharan Africa and other LMICs. Aspiration risk factors include reduced consciousness, swallowing disorders, and alcohol dependence.

**Typical presentation:** CAP typically presents as an acute illness over one to five days with cough, fever, and localising chest signs. Pneumococcal pneumonia (the commonest bacterial cause) has a classic lobar pattern with abrupt-onset rigors and rust-coloured sputum. Atypical pneumonia — caused by Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydophila pneumoniae, or Legionella pneumophila — tends to present more gradually with a dry cough, prominent systemic features, and extrapulmonary manifestations (headache, myalgia, rash, diarrhoea); chest X-ray changes may be more extensive than clinical findings suggest. In HIV-positive individuals and in children, Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP) must be considered — it presents insidiously with progressive dyspnoea, non-productive cough, and hypoxia disproportionate to chest signs.

**Important differential diagnoses:** Pulmonary tuberculosis shares fever and productive cough but follows an insidious course with prominent constitutional weight loss and night sweats over weeks to months, usually without acute lobar consolidation; the absence of antibiotic response should prompt TB investigation. Pulmonary embolism produces pleuritic chest pain and dyspnoea but typically without fever, purulent sputum, or lobar consolidation; tachycardia, hypoxia, and risk factors for venous thromboembolism are key differentiators. Acute decompensated heart failure may produce dyspnoea and bilateral basal crackles with radiological infiltrates resembling pneumonia but without fever or localised consolidation; elevated JVP, peripheral oedema, and response to diuretics help distinguish. Bronchiectasis with acute exacerbation produces purulent sputum and cough but on a background of chronic disease with large sputum volumes; CT demonstrates bronchiectatic changes.

**Features that argue against this diagnosis:** Absence of fever, productive cough, and any respiratory symptoms makes pneumonia an unlikely primary diagnosis. Normal chest X-ray argues against established lobar consolidation, though it does not exclude very early pneumonia in the first 24–48 hours. Bilateral, symmetric radiological changes without fever or localising signs are more consistent with cardiac pulmonary oedema than infective consolidation. Normal oxygen saturation and absence of tachypnoea argue against clinically significant pneumonia. A presentation with prominent systemic features (severe myalgia, retro-orbital headache, rash, thrombocytopenia) without productive cough or localising chest signs makes atypical pneumonia less likely than dengue, malaria, or typhoid in endemic settings.

**Red flags (severity assessment — CURB-65):** One point each for: Confusion (new), Urea >7 mmol/L, Respiratory rate ≥30 breaths/minute, Blood pressure <90 mmHg systolic or ≤60 mmHg diastolic, age ≥65 years. Score ≥3 indicates high mortality risk and requires hospital admission with consideration of ICU-level care. Additional red flags: oxygen saturation <94% on air, bilateral or multi-lobar infiltrates, septic shock, age <2 months (in children). PCP presenting with oxygen saturation <94% and CD4 <200 in an HIV-positive patient requires urgent high-dose co-trimoxazole and adjuvant corticosteroids.

**Diagnostic context:** CAP is primarily a clinical diagnosis. Chest X-ray should be obtained to confirm pneumonic infiltrate, assess extent, and identify complications (effusion, cavity, pneumothorax). HIV testing should be offered to all patients in high-prevalence settings — it changes the differential and management significantly. In moderate-severe CAP, blood cultures (before antibiotics), sputum gram stain and culture, and a pneumococcal urinary antigen test increase aetiological yield. Oxygen saturation measurement is essential for triage. Empirical antibiotic therapy should follow local guidelines and resistance patterns; escalation to broad-spectrum cover is required when atypical, PCP, or drug-resistant organisms are suspected. Failure to respond to treatment within 48–72 hours should prompt reassessment and TB investigation in endemic settings.
