# Peptic ulcer disease

Peptic ulcer disease (PUD) is a mucosal defect penetrating beyond the muscularis mucosae of the stomach (gastric ulcer) or duodenum (duodenal ulcer), resulting primarily from Helicobacter pylori infection or prolonged NSAID/aspirin use, which disrupt the mucosal defence mechanisms against acid injury.

**Cardinal symptoms:** Epigastric pain — typically described as burning, gnawing, or aching — is the hallmark. The temporal relationship between pain and food is the key differentiating clue: duodenal ulcer pain characteristically occurs two to three hours after eating (when buffering by food is exhausted), is relieved by food or antacids, and frequently wakes the patient from sleep in the early hours of the morning. Gastric ulcer pain tends to be precipitated by food rather than relieved by it. Pain is typically recurrent over months to years with periods of remission.

**Associated symptoms and signs:** Nausea and bloating accompany most presentations. Heartburn and waterbrash (reflux of gastric fluid into the mouth) may occur. Vomiting may relieve pain, particularly if gastric outlet obstruction — a complication — is developing. Weight loss from reduced food intake due to pain is more prominent in gastric ulcer than duodenal ulcer. Haematemesis (vomiting of blood or coffee-ground material) and melaena (black, tarry stool) indicate upper gastrointestinal bleeding. Epigastric tenderness to palpation is the main examination finding; peritoneal signs suggest perforation.

**Diagnostic features:** Direct visualisation of a mucosal defect penetrating the muscularis mucosae on upper gastrointestinal endoscopy is the gold standard. Biopsy of any gastric ulcer is mandatory to exclude malignancy — this is a required step, not optional. Positive H. pylori testing — CLO test on biopsy, urea breath test, or stool antigen test — confirms the primary aetiological factor in most cases. Duodenal ulcer biopsy for malignancy is not routinely required. Symptom relief with proton pump inhibitor therapy provides supportive evidence but is insufficient for diagnosis; GERD, functional dyspepsia, and gastric cancer can all respond partially to acid suppression.

**Predisposing factors:** Helicobacter pylori infection — transmitted via the faecal-oral route — is the most important cause globally and particularly prevalent in low-income settings with limited access to clean water and sanitation. NSAID and aspirin use (including low-dose aspirin for cardiovascular prophylaxis) impair prostaglandin synthesis and disrupt mucosal defence. Smoking doubles ulcer risk and delays healing. Heavy alcohol use contributes through direct mucosal irritation. Physiological stress (critical illness, mechanical ventilation, major surgery) causes stress ulcers. Rare causes include Zollinger-Ellison syndrome (gastrinoma producing hypersecretion).

**Typical presentation:** PUD follows a chronic relapsing course. A middle-aged adult with a months-long history of recurrent epigastric burning that is partially relieved by antacids, worse at night (duodenal) or with food (gastric), in the context of H. pylori infection or regular NSAID use, is the characteristic presentation. Many patients present for the first time with a complication — bleeding (haematemesis or melaena), perforation (sudden severe abdominal pain with peritonism), or gastric outlet obstruction (postprandial vomiting of undigested food).

**Important differential diagnoses:** Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GERD) produces epigastric and retrosternal burning, characteristically worse after large meals, on lying flat, and at night; it is relieved by sitting upright and antacids; regurgitation of acid is prominent. Functional dyspepsia mimics PUD symptomatically but endoscopy reveals no ulcer; it is a diagnosis of exclusion. Gastric cancer shares epigastric pain and weight loss but is associated with alarm features — progressive dysphagia, persistent vomiting, palpable epigastric mass, and anaemia — and must be urgently excluded by endoscopy in older adults with new dyspepsia. Acute pancreatitis produces severe epigastric pain radiating to the back, associated with elevated serum amylase or lipase and frequently triggered by alcohol or gallstone disease. Biliary colic is colicky right upper quadrant pain radiating to the right shoulder or scapula, precipitated by fatty meals, with tender right hypochondrium and possible jaundice. Mesenteric ischaemia must be considered when severe abdominal pain is disproportionate to examination findings, particularly in older adults with vascular disease.

**Features that argue against this diagnosis:** Complete and sustained absence of epigastric pain or dyspeptic symptoms makes active peptic ulcer unlikely. Normal upper gastrointestinal endoscopy at the time of symptoms excludes an active peptic ulcer. Predominant retrosternal burning worse on lying flat and after large meals, with regurgitation of acid, is more consistent with GERD than PUD. The presence of alarm features — dysphagia, progressive weight loss, a palpable mass, or persistent vomiting — shifts the priority to excluding gastric malignancy before attributing symptoms to benign PUD. Absence of H. pylori on testing and no NSAID/aspirin use makes PUD substantially less likely, though not impossible.

**Red flags (alarm features requiring urgent endoscopy):** Age over 55 with new-onset dyspepsia. Unintentional weight loss. Persistent vomiting. Progressive dysphagia or odynophagia. Iron deficiency anaemia. Haematemesis or melaena. Palpable epigastric or abdominal mass. Family history of upper gastrointestinal cancer. Previous gastric surgery or confirmed Barrett's oesophagus. Sudden-onset severe epigastric pain with peritonism suggests perforation — a surgical emergency. Haemodynamic instability (hypotension, tachycardia) with haematemesis or melaena requires resuscitation and emergency endoscopy.

**Diagnostic context:** Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy is the gold standard — it directly visualises the ulcer, allows biopsy for malignancy (mandatory for all gastric ulcers) and H. pylori testing (CLO test), and permits therapeutic intervention for bleeding. H. pylori testing should be performed in all patients with PUD. Non-invasive testing options include the urea breath test and stool antigen test, both of which have high sensitivity and specificity. Serology is less reliable due to persistent antibody positivity post-eradication. NSAIDs should be discontinued where possible. H. pylori eradication with a validated first-line regimen is the cornerstone of treatment for H. pylori-positive PUD and significantly reduces ulcer recurrence.
