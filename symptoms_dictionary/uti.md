# Urinary tract infection

A urinary tract infection (UTI) is a microbial invasion of any part of the urinary tract — urethra, bladder (cystitis), ureters, or renal pelvis and parenchyma (pyelonephritis) — most commonly caused by Gram-negative enteric bacteria, of which Escherichia coli accounts for approximately 80% of uncomplicated cases.

**Cardinal symptoms:** Lower UTI (cystitis) presents with dysuria — a burning or stinging sensation during urination — along with urinary frequency and urgency. Suprapubic discomfort or aching is common. Upper UTI (pyelonephritis) adds fever, loin pain (unilateral flank pain radiating to the groin), and costovertebral angle tenderness on examination, reflecting renal parenchymal involvement.

**Associated symptoms and signs:** Haematuria — macroscopic or microscopic — occurs in both lower and upper UTI. Cloudy, offensive-smelling urine suggests significant pyuria. Systemic features — fever, rigors, nausea, and vomiting — are characteristic of pyelonephritis and distinguish it from uncomplicated cystitis. In elderly patients, confusion and functional decline may be the presenting features of a UTI with otherwise minimal urinary symptoms; however, asymptomatic bacteriuria is common in older adults and should not be treated unless specific criteria are met.

**Diagnostic features:** Positive urine dipstick for nitrites (high specificity for gram-negative bacteriuria) and/or leukocyte esterase (reflecting pyuria) is the appropriate first-line test for uncomplicated lower UTI in women. Pyuria on microscopy (≥10 white blood cells per mm³ in unspun urine) confirms urinary tract inflammation. Midstream urine culture demonstrating ≥10⁵ colony-forming units per mL of a single uropathogen is the diagnostic gold standard; lower thresholds (≥10³ CFU/mL) apply for symptomatic women and catheter specimens. Costovertebral angle tenderness on bimanual palpation, combined with fever, supports the diagnosis of pyelonephritis over cystitis.

**Predisposing factors:** Female sex is the most important structural risk factor due to the short urethra and its proximity to the perineum. Sexual intercourse increases exposure to uropathogens. Urinary catheters are the leading cause of healthcare-associated UTI. Structural abnormalities of the urinary tract — stones, strictures, vesicoureteral reflux, polycystic kidney disease — impair urinary flow and promote infection. Diabetes mellitus impairs immune defences and is associated with more complicated UTI. Pregnancy produces urinary stasis through progesterone-mediated ureteral dilatation, making asymptomatic bacteriuria clinically significant in this group. Immunocompromise (HIV, chemotherapy, corticosteroids) predisposes to atypical organisms.

**Typical presentation:** In a healthy, non-pregnant, sexually active woman, uncomplicated cystitis presents as acute dysuria, frequency, and urgency without fever or systemic features. Onset is typically over hours to a day. Pyelonephritis presents more dramatically with loin pain, fever ≥38°C, rigors, and systemic illness, often with a preceding or concurrent lower urinary tract symptoms. In men, a UTI at any age should prompt consideration of structural abnormality or prostatitis, as uncomplicated UTI is uncommon in men without predisposing factors.

**Important differential diagnoses:** Urethritis due to sexually transmitted infections — chlamydia (Chlamydia trachomatis) or gonorrhoea (Neisseria gonorrhoeae) — produces dysuria and urethral discharge or vaginal discharge; it should be considered in sexually active young adults, especially when dipstick is negative or does not explain symptoms. Vaginitis (bacterial vaginosis, candidiasis, trichomoniasis) causes external dysuria from vulval irritation and vaginal discharge; symptoms are worsened by urination over an inflamed vulva rather than internal burning. Bladder and urethral stones cause dysuria and haematuria without infective features; pain may be positional. Overactive bladder syndrome produces urgency and frequency without dysuria or infective markers. Prostatitis in men causes perineal and lower back pain alongside lower urinary tract symptoms; the prostate is tender on digital rectal examination.

**Features that argue against this diagnosis:** Negative nitrite and negative leukocyte esterase on urine dipstick has a high negative predictive value for UTI in a symptomatic woman — both negative together makes bacterial UTI unlikely. Sterile pyuria — white cells present but no bacterial growth on culture — argues against a standard bacterial UTI and raises the possibility of urethritis from STI, renal TB, or interstitial nephritis. External dysuria described as burning over the vulval skin on contact with urine, accompanied by vaginal discharge or itch, is more consistent with vaginitis than cystitis; internal dysuria with urethral burning is the more specific symptom of cystitis. Normal urinalysis and negative culture in a symptomatic patient should prompt consideration of overactive bladder, vulvodynia, or pelvic floor dysfunction. Male sex without a urinary catheter, recent urological procedure, or documented structural abnormality argues against uncomplicated lower UTI — UTI in a man under 50 without a precipitating factor is atypical and should prompt consideration of prostatitis, epididymo-orchitis, or urological investigation rather than simple cystitis.

**Red flags:** Signs of urosepsis — fever with tachycardia, hypotension, or altered consciousness in the context of a UTI — represent a medical emergency requiring immediate parenteral antibiotics, fluid resuscitation, and intensive monitoring. Pyelonephritis in pregnancy carries a high risk of preterm labour and requires inpatient intravenous antibiotic treatment. UTI in a man under 50 without a clear precipitating factor (e.g. catheter) requires urological investigation to exclude structural pathology. Recurrent UTI in a woman — three or more episodes per year — warrants midstream urine culture confirmation, assessment for modifiable risk factors, and consideration of cystoscopy to exclude intravesical pathology. Macroscopic haematuria without infective symptoms requires cystoscopy to exclude bladder malignancy.

**Diagnostic context:** Urine dipstick testing is the appropriate first-line investigation for uncomplicated lower UTI in women — a positive nitrite result has high specificity for bacterial infection; combined nitrites and leukocytes increase sensitivity. Midstream urine (MSU) for microscopy, culture, and sensitivity should be sent before commencing antibiotics in: pyelonephritis, pregnancy, men, catheterised patients, recurrent or complicated UTI, and treatment failure. In sub-Saharan Africa, antibiotic resistance patterns — including extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) producing E. coli — are increasingly prevalent and local susceptibility data should guide empirical choice. Asymptomatic bacteriuria (positive MSU without symptoms) should be treated only in pregnancy and prior to urological procedures; it does not require treatment in catheterised patients, elderly, or diabetic individuals without symptoms.
