# Acute gastroenteritis (infectious)

Acute infectious gastroenteritis is inflammation of the gastrointestinal mucosa — stomach and small or large intestine — caused by viral, bacterial, or parasitic pathogens, producing diarrhoea with or without vomiting, abdominal cramping, and variable systemic features. It is a leading cause of childhood morbidity and mortality globally, and a major burden in settings with limited access to safe water and sanitation.

**Cardinal symptoms:** Diarrhoea — three or more loose or watery stools in a 24-hour period — is the defining feature. Nausea and vomiting typically precede or accompany diarrhoea, particularly in viral causes. Abdominal cramps and bloating are common. Onset is usually acute, over hours to one day.

**Associated symptoms and signs:** Fever is more common and higher in bacterial gastroenteritis, particularly invasive or toxin-producing species, than in viral causes. Dehydration develops progressively — early signs include dry mouth, reduced urine output, and thirst; moderate signs include sunken eyes, reduced skin turgor, and tachycardia; severe signs include sunken eyes, no tears when crying, very slow skin turgor, lethargy or unconsciousness, and absent urine output. Bloody or mucoid stool (dysentery) indicates invasive or colonic bacterial infection. Weight loss occurs with prolonged illness. Borborygmi (loud gurgling bowel sounds) reflect increased intestinal motility.

**Diagnostic features:** The diagnosis is clinical in uncomplicated cases: three or more loose or watery stools within 24 hours with acute onset over hours to two days, with or without vomiting and abdominal cramps. Positive stool culture identifies the causative organism in bacterial gastroenteritis — Salmonella, Shigella, Campylobacter, pathogenic E. coli. Positive rotavirus or norovirus stool antigen or PCR confirms viral aetiology. Faecal leucocytes on microscopy indicate invasive or inflammatory mucosal involvement, consistent with bacterial rather than viral cause. Clinical dehydration grading — mild, moderate, severe — should be formally assessed using WHO or IMCI criteria and documented, as it determines management pathway.

**Predisposing factors:** Contaminated food or water — undercooked meat, unpasteurised dairy, inadequately stored food, faecally contaminated water sources. Poor hand hygiene, open defecation, and inadequate sanitation infrastructure. Close household or institutional contact with an infected person (viral outbreaks, particularly norovirus, spread rapidly in closed settings). Age extremes — under five and elderly individuals — are at highest risk for severe dehydration and complications. Immunocompromise (HIV, malnutrition, corticosteroid use) predisposes to atypical organisms and prolonged illness. Antibiotic use predisposes to Clostridioides difficile infection.

**Typical presentation:** Viral gastroenteritis (rotavirus, norovirus) produces profuse watery diarrhoea, vomiting, and low-grade fever, typically self-limiting within two to five days. Rotavirus is the dominant cause in children under five. Bacterial gastroenteritis — from Salmonella, Campylobacter, Escherichia coli, Shigella — tends to produce higher fever, more abdominal pain, and bloody or mucoid stool (Shigella, Campylobacter) or profuse watery stool without blood (enterotoxigenic E. coli, Vibrio cholerae). Food-borne illness from preformed toxins (Staphylococcus aureus, Bacillus cereus) produces rapid-onset vomiting within one to six hours of a specific food exposure. Cholera produces rice-water diarrhoea of rapid onset with massive fluid losses leading to life-threatening dehydration within hours.

**Important differential diagnoses:** Typhoid fever (enteric fever) shares fever and abdominal symptoms but has an insidious onset over one to two weeks with relative bradycardia, constipation more often than diarrhoea (especially early), rose-coloured spots on the trunk, and splenomegaly; blood culture is the diagnostic test. Malaria in endemic settings commonly produces fever with nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain that can mimic gastroenteritis; blood film or RDT should be performed in any febrile patient in a malaria-endemic area regardless of GI symptoms. Appendicitis presents with anorexia, periumbilical pain migrating to the right iliac fossa, localised tenderness and guarding, and low-grade fever; diarrhoea is uncommon. Inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis) produces recurrent or chronic diarrhoea — often bloody in ulcerative colitis — without an acute infectious trigger; colonoscopy is required for diagnosis. Clostridioides difficile infection should be considered in patients with recent antibiotic use and watery or bloody diarrhoea; stool toxin testing confirms the diagnosis.

**Features that argue against this diagnosis:** Diarrhoea persisting beyond 14 days is by definition not acute gastroenteritis — it is persistent diarrhoea and requires investigation for parasitic infection (Giardia, Cryptosporidium, Entamoeba), inflammatory bowel disease, or malabsorption. Absence of any identifiable infectious exposure — contaminated food or water, contact with a sick individual, institutional setting — in a non-immunocompromised patient weakens the diagnosis and should prompt consideration of non-infectious causes. Localised right iliac fossa pain with anorexia and low-grade fever, without significant diarrhoea, is more consistent with appendicitis than gastroenteritis; this distinction is clinically critical. Recurrent episodic diarrhoea over months without a clear infectious trigger suggests inflammatory bowel disease rather than acute infectious gastroenteritis.

**Red flags:** Severe dehydration — particularly in children under five — requires immediate rehydration and is a life-threatening emergency. Signs: sunken fontanelle (infants), absent tears, sunken eyes, prolonged skin turgor (>2 seconds), lethargy, unconsciousness, absent urine output. Cholera-like presentation with rice-water diarrhoea and rapid dehydration must be treated as cholera until excluded by stool culture. Bloody diarrhoea with fever (dysentery) in a child under five may indicate severe Shigella infection causing haemolytic uraemic syndrome — monitor urine output, renal function, and haematological parameters. Diarrhoea persisting beyond 14 days (persistent diarrhoea) or beyond 30 days (chronic diarrhoea) requires full investigation including HIV testing, stool parasitology, and consideration of inflammatory bowel disease. Haemodynamic compromise — hypotension, tachycardia, oliguria — requires immediate IV fluid resuscitation.

**Diagnostic context:** The diagnosis is clinical in the majority of uncomplicated cases. Stool culture and microscopy should be performed in: bloody diarrhoea, systemic illness or fever, immunocompromised patients, suspected outbreak, diarrhoea persisting beyond 7 days, and recent antibiotic use (to exclude C. difficile). In malaria-endemic settings, a blood film or RDT should be performed on any febrile patient with GI symptoms. Serum electrolytes and creatinine are indicated in moderate-to-severe dehydration to guide rehydration. HIV testing should be offered in high-prevalence settings. Oral rehydration solution (ORS) — WHO/UNICEF low-osmolarity formulation — is the cornerstone of management for mild-to-moderate dehydration in all ages. Zinc supplementation for 10–14 days in children under five reduces severity and duration of diarrhoeal episodes. Antibiotics are not indicated routinely; they are indicated for confirmed or suspected Shigella, cholera, severe salmonellosis in high-risk individuals, and C. difficile.
